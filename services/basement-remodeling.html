Basement Remodeling

Basement Finishing and Remodeling

A basement is the cheapest square footage in the house to finish and the easiest to finish badly. Water and air handling decide which one you get.

Request a Consultation Call 833-637-8466

Solve the Basement Before You Finish It

Finishing a basement is attractive because the structure already exists. The floor, walls and ceiling are in place, and the cost per square foot is well below an addition. That is genuinely true, and it is also why basements get finished before the underlying conditions are addressed.

A basement that takes water occasionally is an inconvenience while it is unfinished. Once it is framed, insulated, drywalled and carpeted, the same amount of water is a demolition project. The same applies to humidity: a basement that stays damp will grow problems behind finished walls that nobody sees until there is an odor.

Praxis Building Group treats moisture and air as the first phase of a basement project, not as an optional upgrade. Everything after that is straightforward.

Typical Project Scope

A typical basement finishing scope includes:

  • Assessment of existing moisture conditions, grading, downspout discharge and any existing drainage or sump system
  • Correction of water entry sources before framing begins
  • Layout planning around existing mechanicals, beams, columns and low points
  • Egress window installation or enlargement where a bedroom is planned
  • Framing of walls, soffits and mechanical chases
  • Sub slab or perimeter insulation appropriate to a below grade assembly
  • Electrical: circuits, lighting layout, receptacles, smoke and carbon monoxide detection
  • Plumbing where a bathroom, wet bar or utility sink is added, including drain considerations below slab
  • HVAC coordination: supply and return for the new space, and dehumidification
  • Drywall, taping and painting
  • Flooring appropriate to below grade conditions
  • Doors, trim, stair finishing and railings
  • Final inspection coordination and punch list

Not every project includes every item. Scope is written for your specific house and stated plainly before pricing.

The Praxis Building Group Approach

Water first

Before anything is framed, we look at where water is coming from: grading that slopes toward the house, downspouts discharging at the foundation, window well drainage, foundation cracks, or hydrostatic pressure requiring a drainage system. Fixing the exterior cause is almost always cheaper than managing the interior symptom.

Assemblies built for below grade

A basement wall is not an above grade wall. It has a concrete or block face that stays cool, and warm interior air that carries moisture toward it. The insulation strategy has to keep that air away from the cold surface. Framing a stud wall tight to bare foundation with fiberglass batts is a common approach and a poor one in Michigan's climate.

Egress where people will sleep

If the plan includes a bedroom, an egress window is a code requirement, not a preference. It also involves excavation, a window well, a properly sized opening cut into the foundation, and drainage for the well. We raise this at the scope stage because it is a meaningful line item that homeowners frequently do not expect.

Mechanicals get planned, not boxed in

Furnaces, water heaters, panels, cleanouts and shutoffs need clearance and access after the basement is finished. Framing that buries a cleanout or blocks a panel creates a problem for the next person who needs it, and can fail inspection.

Construction Considerations

Ceiling height and duct routing

Many Southeast Michigan basements have limited headroom to begin with, and ductwork, beams and plumbing lines reduce it further. Whether ducts can be reworked to gain height is a real question worth asking before the layout is set, because it affects how the finished space feels more than almost any other decision.

Adding a basement bathroom

A basement bathroom is one of the highest value additions to a finished basement and the item most likely to require breaking the slab. Whether gravity drainage is possible depends on the depth of the existing sanitary line. Where it is not, an ejector pump system is the alternative and needs to be planned for, not added late.

Radon

Radon is present in parts of Southeast Michigan and testing is inexpensive. If a basement is going to become occupied living space, testing before finishing is worth doing, because a mitigation system is far easier to install before the space is closed in.

Flooring that tolerates the environment

Below grade slabs move moisture even when they appear dry. Flooring selection should account for that. Solid hardwood directly on a slab is a poor choice. Several resilient and engineered options are appropriate and Praxis Building Group will steer the selection accordingly.

Planning This Project

The largest cost variables in a basement are whether water issues need correcting, whether an egress window is required, and whether a bathroom is included. A dry basement with adequate headroom and no bathroom is one of the most cost effective ways to add usable space to a Michigan home.

It is worth deciding early how the space will actually be used. A basement planned as one open room is different from one planned as a bedroom, bathroom and office, and the difference shows up in egress, mechanical, electrical and permit requirements rather than in the finishes.

Questions

Questions About Basement Remodeling

My basement gets a little water once or twice a year. Can it still be finished?

Not until the water is dealt with. A basement that takes water occasionally will keep doing it, and the cost of that water goes up by a large multiple once the space is finished. In most cases the source is exterior and correctable: grading, downspout discharge, window wells or a foundation crack. Praxis Building Group would rather spend a portion of the budget on that first than finish over it.

Do I need an egress window?

If any part of the finished basement is going to be used as a bedroom, yes. Egress requirements exist so someone can get out and a firefighter can get in. Installing one involves excavation, cutting the foundation, a properly sized window and a drained window well. If no bedroom is planned, egress may not be required, but it is still worth discussing.

Can I add a bathroom to my basement?

Usually. The question is whether the existing sanitary line is deep enough for gravity drainage from the new fixtures. If it is, the slab is cut, drain lines are run and the slab is patched. If it is not, an ejector pump system handles it. Either is workable, but they are different in cost and need to be determined before the layout is finalized.

How much headroom do I need?

Michigan residential code sets a minimum finished ceiling height for habitable basement space, with allowances for beams and ducts at intervals. We measure the actual conditions during the walkthrough. Where the existing height is marginal, rerouting ductwork can sometimes recover several inches, and that is a decision worth making before framing.
