Home Additions

Home Additions in Southeast Michigan

An addition is new construction attached to an old building. The hard part is the seam between them.

Request a Consultation Call 833-637-8466

Adding Space Without Making the House Look Added To

Building an addition is a well understood construction problem. Foundation, framing, roof, envelope, systems, finishes. The difficulty is that it has to meet a house that was built to different standards, has settled for decades, and has a roofline, siding and window pattern that the new work has to resolve against.

A poorly executed addition announces itself immediately: rooflines that do not relate, a floor that steps unexpectedly, siding that does not match, windows at a different head height. Those are all decisions made before anything is built.

Praxis approaches an addition as two problems solved together: the structure itself, and how it joins what is already there.

Typical Project Scope

A typical addition includes:

  • Site evaluation, setback and zoning review, and confirmation of what the lot permits
  • Drawings and engineering as required for permit
  • Excavation, footings and foundation, whether full basement, crawl space or slab
  • Waterproofing, drainage and backfill at the new foundation
  • Floor system framing tied into the existing structure at a matched elevation
  • Wall and roof framing, including the tie in to the existing roof
  • Roofing, flashing and integration with the existing roof plane
  • Windows and exterior doors selected to relate to the existing house
  • Siding, trim and exterior detailing matched to the existing exterior
  • Electrical service evaluation and extension of circuits to the new space
  • Plumbing extension where the addition includes fixtures
  • HVAC capacity evaluation and distribution to the new space
  • Insulation and air sealing to current code
  • Drywall, flooring, trim, doors, paint and finishes
  • Removal of the existing exterior wall where the addition opens into the house
  • Punch list and final inspections

Not every project includes every item. Scope is written for your specific house and stated plainly before pricing.

The Praxis Approach

Zoning before design

Setbacks, lot coverage, height limits and easements determine what can be built before any design question is worth discussing. Praxis confirms those constraints with the local jurisdiction early, because designing an addition the zoning will not allow wastes everyone's time.

Matching elevations

A new floor system has to land at the same elevation as the existing one, accounting for different joist depths, different subfloor thicknesses and decades of settlement in the original structure. A step between old and new is sometimes a design choice and more often a planning failure.

The roof tie in is the critical detail

Where a new roof meets an existing one is where water will find any mistake. The valley, the flashing, the underlayment lap and the framing that supports it all have to be executed correctly, and the geometry has to be resolved before framing rather than improvised on the roof.

Matching the existing exterior honestly

Siding profiles, brick, trim details and window styles from decades past are not always still available. Praxis will tell you when an exact match is not achievable and propose a deliberate approach instead, such as a defined transition, rather than an approximate match that reads as a mistake.

Construction Considerations

Second story additions

Adding a second story is structurally the most demanding addition type. The existing foundation and first floor walls have to carry the new load, which often requires reinforcement. It also means the house is open to weather during part of the work, which drives both sequencing and risk management.

Bump outs and cantilevers

Small additions of a few feet can sometimes be cantilevered off the existing floor system, avoiding foundation work entirely. Whether that is possible depends on joist direction, span and the load being added. Where it works, it is a significant cost saving.

HVAC capacity

An addition adds conditioned volume. Extending existing ductwork into a new space without confirming the system can handle it produces a room that is cold in January and a house that is less comfortable overall. Load calculation belongs in the planning stage.

Utilities and site conditions

Septic fields, wells, buried utilities, easements and mature trees all constrain where a foundation can go. In Livingston County and rural Oakland County in particular, septic location is frequently the deciding factor in where an addition is placed.

Planning This Project

Additions carry a longer preconstruction phase than most remodeling work, because drawings, engineering, zoning review and permitting all happen before any work is visible. Building that time into your expectations prevents the impression that nothing is happening.

The cost per square foot of an addition is higher than finishing existing space, because it includes foundation, framing, roof and envelope. Where the goal is more usable space rather than specifically more footprint, it is worth comparing an addition against finishing a basement or reworking the existing floor plan.

Questions

Questions About Home Additions

How do I know if my lot allows an addition?

Zoning determines it: setbacks from each property line, maximum lot coverage, height limits, and any easements recorded on the property. Those vary by municipality and sometimes by parcel. Praxis confirms them with the local jurisdiction before design work begins, because it defines the envelope everything else fits inside.

Will the addition match the rest of the house?

That is the goal, and how closely depends on the existing materials. Brick, certain siding profiles and older window styles are not always still manufactured. Where an exact match is not possible, the better answer is a deliberate transition rather than a near miss, and Praxis will show you the options before committing.

Can we stay in the house during an addition?

Usually yes, and more comfortably than during an interior renovation. Most of the work happens outside the existing envelope. The disruptive period is when the existing exterior wall is opened to connect the two, which is typically brief and planned for. Second story additions are the exception.

Is an addition cheaper than moving?

Sometimes, and it depends on more than construction cost. An addition avoids transaction costs, keeps a location and a school district, and improves a house you already know. It also carries a limit: there is a point at which a house cannot support the value being added to it. That is worth a conversation with a real estate professional alongside a construction estimate.
